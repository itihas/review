bar stories

🌱 tags: inkhaven updated: 2026-11-26 Thu 02:58, 2026-11-26 Thu 02:55

I've gotten decent at telling bar stories over the years. This reminds me, I might say, of the time they put Kepler's mom on trial for witchcraft. Yeah, no, that happened! Yeah, the fucking Church, man – well, see the interesting thing is that I don't even think it was all that coordinated or anything, she was just separately a woman alone and financially independent, and, like, some lady reported her and the guy who ran the town really liked burning witches, you know? Now that's a goddamn antifragile system. Well, is it antifragile or just distributed? No, neither of those…it's more like the thing where everybody on the Continent just decided to coordinate on a delusion at once, and that's actually an incredibly powerful social technology if you think about it, right? They had that social order locked down. I wonder what the formal property corresponding to that would be…

It's fun. I like doing this kind of exposition. You can listen for the bit the other person's interested in, and when they don't know something, well, there's a whole other story to tell. What was Kepler even doing, they might ask? Was he before or after Galileo? So which thing did which guy figure out exactly? The phones come out and start showing Wikipedia. Now we're all doing my favorite thing in the world to do. A grand time will be had.

Turning this into the skill where you write these things down involves some amount of bullshit hard work that I'm currently both annoyed by and excited by the prospect of. You don't get to hear what the other person is or isn't interested in, or does and doesn't know. You don't get to know until much later whether anyone's had any fun, or has any questions. And when you write something down, it's serious. You have to have your details kept decently straight to tell a good bar story, sure – if you stumble too much, people get bored. But the standard for writing is just that tick higher. You're asking for more attention. You're expected to give an actual sense of understanding something, not just sing the rhyming bits of the thing at people. You can't expect people to come in with a bank of the specific big fuzzy concept-handles that would make this whole thing possible in a six word sentence. You have to spell it all out.

But then, I did have to get good at telling bar stories on purpose. At that time, I'd have written something like:

The problem goes like this, see: every so often, maybe every half hour or so, (I do mean this happens all the time), an orb appears before me.

🔵

It has scratches on the surface; cloudy little spots where it's been rested ungently on or against surfaces, and long dramatic streaks where a plot moment occurred.

Through the orb, the world looks different. Darker, often inverted, bloated. The laughter of the people around me diminishes them to nothing, or stretches their mouths around the orb's belly, teeth prominent and pointing outwards.

Inside those mouths, strange shapes flutter. I know them well, or think I do; I think of them as friends.

I'd like to introduce them round, smile and backslap and herald their arrival; they remain as they always do in the orb, and other people have a hard time noticing that they're there.

If I spend too long talking about my friends in the orb, several undesirable situations ensue. Their existence is held dubious, their welcome more dubious still; sometimes people don't even notice that the orb itself has appeared on the table, or think its appearance irrelevant.

For many years, this confused and upset me. I eventually realized, however, that all you have to do to get people to see the orb, and notice your friends, is to speak as if they are there, without ever calling on people to notice that they actually are.

So I tell people about my friends as if I'm telling a story.

And when the story is too mysterious or circuitous, it seems like it pisses them off. But when I try to get into the details, their eyes glaze over. I can't put my thoughts in order, and when I try to start speaking I realize that I don't know what I'm trying to say quite as well as I think I did. My friends gaze out at me, but I wonder, even as I'm looking at them, if they're really there.

I feel alone, and like a traitor, and a failure. I feel like I'm speaking a different language than everybody around me, and like nobody bothered to tell me, and I had to figure it out for myself.

What I realize now is that I was alone, and sad, and thought of my ideas as my friends. Now I have human friends, and my ideas and I have an amicable working relationship. I think they moved out of the orb, perhaps to somewhere more permeable. And I figured out how to tell bar stories; turns out the trick to those is just to like listening.

You know? Just, listen for what it is they're interested in. Which bit confused them? Which bit intrigued them? What makes them light up? When they have more fun, you have more fun: given this, are you telling them a story about penguins or about train lines or about old Sanskrit plays? Is the story going to be a fucked-up one or a squishy happy one or one with a really interesting structure to it? You listen, you pick, and you pivot; and you do this over and over, within one story and across many of them, until you know how to make your friends laugh, or smile, or slow down and check in with themselves, or get caught up in thinking about a problem.

Listening is a bit trickier when writing. But a similar intuition perhaps applies. I may just be over-fascinated by an orb. I may need to figure out how to write in a way that listens.

I think this is an okay place to start thinking about the beating heart that one wants to place inside explainers.
