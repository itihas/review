Game night

Imagine it's board game night. You and your friends want to play a game, but you don't yet know which.

As may happen in some circles, someone says, "Wait. Why don't we make a game out of picking the game? We'll take turns picking. Rock-paper-scissors to determine turn order."

As may happen in some circles, your first player says, "let's play the game-picking game! Same turn order as the above."

This is deemed hilarious, roundly praised as a grand joke.

After a few rounds of selecting the game-picking game, a real game gets chosen.

What is a "real" game? Why?

Hypergame

Hypergame is a mathematical puzzle first described by Zwicker1 to a class of students as a riff on an old exam bonus question:

  • define a "finite game" as a game with no infinite runs; i.e. any given turn might have you selecting among infinite moves, but there will only ever be a finite number of turns.
  • Define hypergame as a game where the first player chooses a finite game to play. All finite games are allowed.

Is "hypergame" a finite game? In other words: can you choose "hypergame" in the first turn of hypergame?

If we chose to play hypergame forever, hypergame would never end. That means it isn't a finite game at all, and shouldn't have been among the choices.

So if we eliminate "hypergame" from the choices of game in hypergame, do we make it finite? Russell's paradox style?

It does not: you've just constructed "hypergame except you can't choose 'hypergame'", which is a /different game than hypergame. Let's call it hypergame-one. "Hypergame-one" now one of the games you can choose to play in hypergame-one. For that matter, "hypergame-one" is also among the choosable games in hypergame! And so is hypergame-two (where you can choose any finite game but can't choose 'hypergame' or 'hypergame-one')

The Zwicker paper shows us how hypergame is a variant of the Burali-Forti paradox, which loosely says that the ordinal numbers can't form a set on pain of antinomy. The analogy goes that choosing is counting: each turn bumps the counter.

There: now I've given you a plausible-enough just-so story, and derived from it a contrived spherical cow. High-decouplers rejoice.2 You are never leaving game night again.

Social choice functions

You know that old adage that "there's enough food in the world for nobody to go hungry – it's just a matter of distribution?"

Let's try to solve a piece of that here.

Abstract away, if you will, the challenges of getting everything everywhere; imagine an Oracle that will fulfill any distribution of resources you choose, just as soon as you can manage to all choose it. One time, one wish. Rezone the farms, earmark the grocery bags, end to end.

Any function that solves for this is called a social choice function. If performed in the style we were all excited about back in the 30s and 40s, it would be a Bergson-Samuelson social welfare function.

Anyway, we can't write these. Or, well, we can, but we won't all ratify them. Or, well, maybe we will, but if

  • We're trying to make sure that we make all choices that would make everybody happier, i.e. nobody's forgetting ethical chocolate while ethical chocolate's on the cards.
  • We're trying to write an allocation of everything we can; we must know what to do with the Nesquik and the durian alike. No exclusions.
  • Relatedly: we're trying not to flip how apples are allocated relative to oranges when we try to introduce the question of how to allocate bananas. This is finicky and horrible but can you think of a better way to update the function without getting Dutch-booked by a bad actor? Remember that we all have to ratify this.3
  • No one person is calling all the shots. No dictators.

Well, we can't. No such function exists.4, 5

Ranked choice voting

You watch Alaska's 2026 midterms with breathless envy. If only, you think, I could vote under a system this good. If only I could vote five times on one ballot. If only I could watch myself converse with ten thousand others – or a hundred million, or two billion – as our second preferences were considered, when our first failed the test of naive consensus. First-past-the-post is a pretty nice way to say my way or the highway. It's a pretty nice way to say my job is not to worry about those people.

There's a system out there, in use today, that does on the merits just seem better.

Locally rational

You go to the market. Tangerines are in season. You weren't craving them before, but now suddenly the dream of a slice, splitting between your teeth, fills your mouth. You replace the orange juice on the shelf from your basket. Five minutes ago, you were debating whether you needed the chocolate really, but now the chocolate can stay.

Tonight's dessert is taking shape in your mind as you cross to the checkout, humming.
