normativity

The concept space of norms and things to do with norms. "Normativity" can refer to

  1. descriptions of norms that are active in your object of study
  2. normative claims you are making about your object of study
  3. what a norm even is

In economics, normativity(2) is a mode of inquiry alongside descriptive and prescriptive modes. It's the class of questions of any of the following forms:

  • "what ought to be?"
  • "what events, were they to transpire, would universally be agreed to be somehow aberrant, and why? what ought to happen in those events?"
  • "what rules or values benefit everybody to implement?"
  • "what rules or values are enforced by everyone?"
  • "what is held to be true by all players in this system, even though the world itself does not seem to offer evidence?"

Q: You may notice that those are all very different types of question, that should return different types of answer. Why do they all fall get called "normative"? Why, actually, am I calling them all normativity(2)?

A: Norms are social truths. Whether by enforcement, equilibrium, or common traits among actors in a system, some things that could in theory happen just don't.

When asked, the answers any embedded explainer will reach for will insult, dismiss, or make an enemy of even a hypothetical violator of a norm.

Normativity is an understudied concept relative to the use economists put it to. From my favorite perspective it's a broad class of examples of mutually enforced reality pruning.

The normative bait-and-switch

An operation I've sometimes seen performed in economics metatheory, where a descriptive hypothesis that gets disproven in an unsatisfying edge-case-y way, or isn't falsifiable in the first place, will sometimes be silently recast as a norm so that it can be kept in the model. Axioms of revealed preference were where I first saw this happen. Properties of the given setting's homo rationalis are also likely suspects.
