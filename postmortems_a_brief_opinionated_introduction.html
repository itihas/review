postmortems: a brief, opinionated introduction

🌰 tags: inkhaven explainerposting

So three days ago ([2026-11-25 Wed]), I realized that I'd booked my flight ticket out from SFO for the 9th of December. This was a problem, I realized, because I'm acting in a performance of Chekhov's The Proposal, directed by the excellent E Baker, scheduled for the 11th. I found this out through a Substack note she had posted.

I spent the first five minutes after seeing that note realizing that

  • I was going to have to reschedule again (I'd already done it once because I'd gotten the dates for Inkhaven wrong).
  • Pre-Christmas tickets were ruinously expensive, and Japan Airlines only had one rescheduling option to offer me, involving a 14 hour layover in Boston.
  • I needed to be back in India in time to apply for my UK visa for MATS, which meant that I couldn't just beg a couch from friends or family for the season.

I then spent two hours after that asking myself and my smarter friend how it had come to this, again.

This could have been rumination. I have extensive notes in my Claude system prompt about what it looks like when I'm ruminating, and what to tell me about it. Of course I immediately find myself rationalizing what I'm doing instead of listening to it whenever this comes up: but I do take a breath when it tells me to take a breath, because nothing true dissolves when you breathe about it. So I breathe-and-rationalize, and underneath all the panicky self-explanation I often find a drive that I actually do care about.

What's behind the question of "why has it once more come to this?"

Well…nothing. When asked in isolation, when one finds the room in one's head not to retreat into a flinch-away, into self-recrimination or fearful rosarying of pasts and futures, there are two simple things happening:

  • I care about this and I care that it's costing me something to mess it up.
  • I actually want to know what's going wrong.1

blameless postmortems

I want to write something sometime soon about the beating heart behind many kinds of corporate paperwork. Bureaucrats, you might be surprised to find, are humans. Lots of what they do has to be shaped around their fallible squishy meatbag selves for the larger apparatus of the Bureaucracy to work.

Among my favorite forms of form is the blameless postmortem. Originally a Google SRE invention, the blameless postmortem is a surprisingly humane document, designed to serve several purposes with a single intention behind them.

It goes like this: you push an update to a caching layer, and Gmail goes down.

If you raise your hand and say, "it might have been the change I just made that took Gmail down," people can check that change, roll it back, and bring it back up in fifteen minutes.

If you don't, people will have to check every change that happened in the last three hours, form competing hypotheses, and test each one. In the process they might break even more things, that will also have to be fixed. Gmail might not be back up for hours.

How do you make it safe for the guy that pushed the breaking change to raise his hand?

blame

The answer is very easy and surprisingly unintuitive: you can't do blame.

Maybe it's not that unintuitive at first blush. When I first read this ethos in a corporate setting, it made immediate sense to me. Laid out socially like this it seems completely obvious that if you make that guy pay for breaking something, you make everybody vigilant, defensive, unwilling to speak baldly about the truth. Letting people get away with doing their jobs is the same thing as letting them do their jobs at all.

The thing is I hadn't realized at the time is that, outside of my own notice, I was thinking something like, well, of course, exceptions aside.

I wonder what your exceptions are to blamelessness. Mine would probably have been quite poorly articulated if they'd risen to the point of articulability in my awareness. I might have said, sometimes people really are at fault; but it's good for some people to get away with things if it means that most people attain positive freedoms. I might have said, sometimes people should take responsibility, and maybe something more is in that one; but in the context of a megacorporation employing thousands of people, nobody should be held responsible for the whole machine when their particular gear's number happened to come up that day.

It's a funny principle, really. When you try to treat "blame is counterproductive" as if it were 100% applicable all the time, no exceptions, it becomes significantly more frustrating: less intuitive, yet somehow no less true.

Even when someone really did take an action that caused an outcome that hurts you, that denies you something you want: blaming them does little to nothing. It carries information about who to ostracize, who to demote in status, who to suspect in future scenarios. It's a limited, lossy heuristic, not even approximately true most of the time. You want your intuitions to attach to something that represents reality better than how blame does.

A person, in 2026, will almost never be anything but a proximate cause. To find a root cause, you look for something weirder, only visible as pattern, only cognitively captured by writing an abstraction over causal space; and you treat that pattern as moving through the person. To attach your intuitions to what has happened more accurately, you have to attach them to the root cause, because the root cause is the invariant, where the person it's moving through will change each time.

Fortunately, intuitions are just emotions, and emotions are incredibly fine-grained when you exmaine them closely. There is a rearrangement available of the moving parts that make up blame.

Here is what is inside blame, as best as I can tell: grief.

Blame is a social inference machine saying:

  • I care about this and I care that it's costing me something to mess it up.
  • I actually want to know what's going wrong.

—and, crucially, flinching. Recrimination, false consequence, fearful rosarying of pasts and futures.

An illustration

My flight error rates

Of the flights I've taken in the last year, 36% of them had something go wrong. Mostly these are day-of incidents, unlike this latest one, which is a booking error. It's also a more esoteric booking error than my last one: instead of straightforwardly getting a known-and-publicized date wrong, I failed to check for an implicit constraint before scheduling.

My mitigation strategy for this quite-high error rate costs me something like 25% more on average per ticket, not counting time to replan and rebook things and time taken (both on the work clock and the calendar(which costs me in fare drift)) to get my booking preview reviewed before I buy.

I'd like to allocate less of my time and money to this problem. I care about this and I don't like how much it costs me to mess it up.

This incident's postmortem

executive summary

A flight out was booked and then a role in a play was accepted, that happened after the date of departure. Upon noticing this information, the flight's date was changed to be after the date of the play.

event log

  • On the 15th of November, I decided to fly out on 9th December, before the formal end of Inkhaven, and booked my return ticket accordingly.
  • I later participated in auditions for an event that was due to happen at the end of Inkhaven, and was accepted for the part.
  • I noticed on the 25th of November from a Substack note that I'd made a commitment to be in Berkeley past the date I was due to leave.
  • I messaged the director to precommit to a ticket change, making sure somebody knew about the problem. I also messaged friends so they'd ask me about it.
  • I spent a few hours with Claude pulling all my flight data from the past year to actually get a sense of how bad this problem was.
  • I paid an elevated fare difference to change my ticket to the 13th. The ticket change was confirmed on the 27th.

impact

  • 42k INR. Per my SLO2, that's my error budget for the six-month window gone. We're now in freeze-mode – do what works, make no changes unless clearly obviously Pareto improvements – until the end of March.
  • Maybe 30 minutes total of time spent exploring my options, finding the alternative flight, getting the booking reviewed.
  • Something like 2 hours of my time spent upset.

root cause

I hadn't noticed how bad a problem this was until this incident. I had an implicit playbook, not an explicit one. I've made an explicit one in the wake of this, just in case it works better; we'll see how useful it ends up being.

I was satisfied with the implicit model because implicit models tend to work better for me. Knowing something off by heart and being able to update my strategy and break my rules on the fly is OP. Also it sucks to have to be rate-limited by the slow, serial process of updating a spreadsheet (or an app that could've been a spreadsheet).

When the implicit-model metastrategy goes wrong, it usually means I'm having a skill issue of some kind.

What am I missing? Date constraints, especially when they're implied rather than stated outright. Why don't implied date constraints make it into my picture of the world? There's something about how I imagine time that isn't working.

For now, let's call this missing skill time anaphora: the ability to resolve events as if they were references to the picture I have of what is happening (has happened, will happen, will not happen).

What went well

  • Precommitments worked; they ensured the problem remained salient in my mind.
  • I found the new tickets and costed everything out quickly.
  • I had a clear picture of my priorities and this made decisionmaking easy.
  • I noticed the discrepancy when presented with the information in the correct format.

Where we got lucky

  • If I hadn't seen the Substack note, I might not have noticed until much later.
  • Japan Airlines prefers to sell out rather than gouge, and this means that I could was very lucky to have a return flight available for rebooking at all.
  • The paranoia about travel going wrong was in this case well-calibrated and functional. No hyper in this vigilance this time.

What could have gone better

  • I had a pretty strong revealed preference to be here until the end of Inkhaven, that I could have priced for myself earlier on.
  • I could have asked after the dates.
  • I could have not auditioned, which while sad would have been more responsible. Or just more cautious. Do I want to have been cautious in this instance? Perhaps not. This may all just be a flinch: updating hurts sometimes. It's possible that this is just what it costs sometimes to get everything you want.

Next steps

  • I'm going to have a 44h journey and will be wiped when I land. I'm going to have to set up some serious recovery plans for when I land. Preliminary notes: white tea, clean laundry, zero-effort low-challenge meal plan, time with friends, TV show queued up to stare at mindlessly for a few hours.
  • I should probably schedule my visa biometric appointment in advance, for a few days after I land, so that I don't have to do that task in the recovery window, and to give me an ironclad reason to climb out of bed eventually and see the sun, because that helps.
  • The spreadsheet I ended up dumping the flight incident data into could stand to become a proper vibed-up app or website for me to keep logging into, because nothing helps you get motivated to fix a problem like an SLO to get number-go-up about.
  • generally speaking, I tend to transition into implicit models after using explicit ones as a crutch. This is how Gutcraft ended up going: it gave me a language to talk about my gut problems in, and that was most of what I ended up keeping around. Meanwhile the optimization pressure that it could have been placing on me just withered away naturally. I wonder whether that's a bug or a feature.

the form, and the function it serves

With some elisions and personal riffs, this is more or less the form in which I've written up dozens of these little guys. Let's talk about the form of the form3 for a second.

Passive voice, correctly reviled in many cases, is appropriate and important here, because it keeps the guy that did it away from the focus. The human brain is always, always looking for social information to anchor its situational understanding to; you have to take Certain Measures to keep a reader focused on what happened instead of who did it.4

We have a summary up top to remind us of what this even is.

We have an event log, usually with a timeline (which I'm a little too lazy for at the moment), with as little editorializing and analysis as possible, because if the analysis is wrong in some way, well, we want something more like an account of the ground truth to anchor our revised analysis to.

"Root cause" is the correct home for analysis. Separated out like this, it helps me notice that analysis is actually hard in like a base, irreducible way. What do I think happened? In this case I notice something like time anaphora. I don't currently imagine time in a way that answers this class of questions. What do I do about that? Well, think about it some more. It's hard. I don't see all the way around it yet. This section is the place where valuable theories are built; it's one of the most valuable pieces of the whole exercise, and I usually fill it in last, because the entire rest of the document is an excuse to lay out the pieces of the puzzle that "root cause" is cuing me to solve.

That's what I think of the remaining pieces as, anyway. Writing prompts that help me get the facts of the matter out; the stuff that will keep circling in me forever if I don't get it down. If I have a place to write "what went well", I won't feel the strange half-shame of being proud of myself for how I handled a situation created by my own mistake; the situation nd the mistake and my handling of it all stop being about me, and I can just describe the pieces of the whole thing I'm happy about, take a second to notice what's working, anchor it in my affective reasoning as things I like and want more of.

Luck is similar. Lots of luck isn't really luck. It's more like grace – like the universe being okay to you at least as often as it isn't okay to you, fractally down inside your mistakes and your recoveries and your mastery and your heroism and all of it. There will be things that happen that you couldn't see coming; and inside the frame of an explanation of one of the bad things, there will be good things.

Impact is where I get to operationalize my grief. What sucked about this in particular? What did it cost me?

"Next steps" is just where I put my todo. The ladder I can use to climb out of the place where this is the thing that's happening, into the rest of my life. The curtains opening and the world through the window, where this is about to become the past, and I get to orient to what happens next. (I love the word next. I think that makes me very Silicon-Valley-core in some ways.)

So that's it, that's the map. Describe it; give it a little time and space and consideration; once you have, take a look into its beating heart, its center, and make what sense of it you can.

And once you have all that written down, you have a picture of a thing that happened to you that you can use to be truthful and clear and also be kind.

Like I said: postmortems are a very humane document.
